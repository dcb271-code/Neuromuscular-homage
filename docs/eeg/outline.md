# Pediatric EEG Reading Curriculum

2026-09-30 · @Dylan Brock

Twelve self-paced modules take a child neurology resident from electrodes and montages to reading a neonatal or PICU record and writing the report. Each module pairs short teaching notes with the best free material from Learning EEG, eeg-training.com and EEGmaster, plus practice questions and a sign-off.

## How to use this curriculum

Work through Modules 1 to 5 in order, then take the neonatal pair (6 and 7) and the abnormal-EEG sequence (8 to 10) in either order, and finish with Module 11. Module 12 runs alongside everything: residents pre-read real studies from the first week and compare them with the attending read.

Each module takes roughly 2 to 4 hours of self-study, plus reading real records. These are estimates, not tested times. Every module has the same parts:

- **Objectives:** what the resident should be able to do.
- **Teaching notes:** the core facts, with pediatric specifics and numbers.
- **Read and watch:** the best free material for that topic.
- **Practice:** short questions, with answers underneath.
- **Sign-off:** what a faculty reader checks before the module counts as done.

**Why it is structured this way.** In a 2024 survey of US and Canadian child neurology program directors, 62% used no objective EEG competency measure and 93% set no minimum number of reads (Katyal 2024). In an online routine-EEG exam, residents scored 72% against experts' 82%. The authors concluded that residents need more than 8 weeks, ideally more than 12, of EEG training for minimal routine-EEG competency (Nascimento 2023, as summarized in Katyal 2024).

**How it maps to ACGME Child Neurology Milestones 2.0 (Patient Care 7, EEG).**

| Milestone level | Wording (ACGME) | Modules |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | Describes general indications for an EEG | 2 |
| Level 2 | Describes normal EEG features using correct terminology, including common artifacts, across the lifespan | 1 to 4 |
| Level 3 | Describes patterns of status epilepticus, normal EEG variants and common abnormalities, across the lifespan | 5, 8, 10 |
| Level 4 | Interprets common EEG abnormalities and creates a report | 9, 12 |
| Level 5 | Interprets uncommon EEG abnormalities | 7, 11 |

The ILAE competency roadmap puts the same skills at its Level 2 (proficiency), including background and sleep at all ages, artifacts and normal variants, and interictal and ictal patterns (Blümcke 2019).

## Module 1. Signals, montages and technical settings

Every later judgment depends on reading polarity and field correctly, so this module comes first and is signed off on localization, not recall.

**Objectives**

- Explain how cortical dipoles produce scalp potentials, and the negative-up display convention.
- Localize a discharge by phase reversal in a bipolar chain and by maximum amplitude in a referential montage.
- Predict how changing the low-frequency filter, high-frequency filter, notch filter, sensitivity and time base changes the tracing.
- Name what differs when recording a neonate or young child.

**Teaching notes**

- **Polarity.** Each channel shows input 1 minus input 2, with negative up. A negative phase reversal, where deflections in adjacent bipolar channels point toward each other, marks the shared electrode as the maximum.
- **End of chain.** A maximum at the end of a bipolar chain (Fp1, Fp2, O1 or O2 in a longitudinal montage) cannot phase-reverse. Check it in a referential montage or a transverse chain.
- **Filters.** Raising the low-frequency filter attenuates slow activity, so focal delta can shrink. Lowering the high-frequency filter smooths muscle into sharply contoured waves that can look like spikes.
- **Time base.** Neonatal records are often reviewed at a slower paper speed than routine records, which compresses waveforms. Always check the page duration before judging frequency.
- **Neonates.** ACNS recommends a 10-20 array modified for neonates, with ECG and respiration channels. EOG and chin EMG are often useful for staging sleep but are not universally required (Shellhaas 2011).
- **Children.** ACNS Guideline 5 sets minimum technical standards for pediatric EEG (Kuratani 2016). Read it once, so you know what a technically adequate pediatric study looks like.

**Read and watch**

- Learning EEG, [Physiology & Terminology](https://www.learningeeg.com/physiology-terminology.html) and [10-20 & Montages](https://www.learningeeg.com/montages.html).
- eeg-training.com, [EEG Fundamentals](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg-fundamentals.html): the signal physiology, 10-20 and montage, and recording-technology units, plus the bipolar math simulator, montage comparison lab and filter toggles.
- [eeg-training.com EEG Videos](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg_videos.html): the polarity and montage lessons, from the Moeller video curriculum (Moeller 2017).
- ACNS [guidelines index](https://www.acns.org/practice/guidelines): Guideline 1 (minimum technical requirements), Guideline 2 (electrode nomenclature) and Guideline 5 (pediatric standards).

**Practice**

1. In the chain Fp1-F3, F3-C3, C3-P3, a sharp transient deflects downward in F3-C3 and upward in C3-P3. Where is its negative maximum?
2. A resident lowers the high-frequency filter from 70 Hz to 15 Hz to "clean up" a record full of muscle artifact. What is the risk?
3. A sharp wave appears only in T5-O1 in a longitudinal bipolar montage, with no phase reversal. What do you do next?

*Answers.* (1) C3: the deflections point toward each other, a negative phase reversal at the shared electrode. (2) Filtered muscle potentials can look like spikes or sharp waves. (3) Review it in a referential montage, because an end-of-chain maximum cannot phase-reverse.

**Sign-off:** the resident localizes five discharges on real records, in both bipolar and referential montages, with a faculty reader.

## Module 2. A systematic read and the report

A fixed reading order prevents the commonest resident error, which is jumping to the one abnormal-looking wave before describing the background.

**Objectives**

- State when an EEG is indicated and what a single study can and cannot answer.
- Read every record in the same order and describe it in standard terms before interpreting it.
- Write a report whose description, impression and clinical correlation are kept separate.

**Teaching notes**

1. **Before the tracing:** age (postmenstrual age for neonates), indication, medications and sedation, and the state the child was in.
2. **Background:** posterior dominant rhythm frequency and reactivity, symmetry, continuity, amplitude and the anterior-to-posterior gradient.
3. **Sleep:** whether it was reached, and whether spindles, vertex waves and other sleep features are present and symmetric for age.
4. **Activation:** whether hyperventilation and photic stimulation were done, and done adequately.
5. **Abnormalities:** location, morphology, prevalence and state, in ACNS or IFCN terms.
6. **Impression:** normal or abnormal, then a numbered list of findings.
7. **Clinical correlation:** what the findings mean for this child, stated as support, not diagnosis.

- A normal routine EEG does not exclude epilepsy. An interictal discharge does not by itself make the diagnosis (see Module 5).
- Record whether hyperventilation was adequate. In an untreated child, 3 minutes of well-performed hyperventilation without 2.5 to 4 Hz spike-wave argues against childhood absence epilepsy (Hirsch E 2022).
- ACNS Guideline 7 sets out report structure (Tatum 2016). The IFCN glossary gives the standard vocabulary and a report format (Kane 2017). SCORE is a structured, computer-based alternative (Beniczky 2017).

**Read and watch**

- eeg-training.com, [EEG Fundamentals](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg-fundamentals.html): the description-language and report-writing units, including the model report.
- eeg-training.com, [ACNS Terminology](https://www.eeg-training.com/acns_criteria.html): the report writer tool.
- [IFCN revised glossary (Kane 2017)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cnp.2017.07.002), free full text.

**Practice**

1. Which belongs in the description and which in the impression: "sharp waves at T3" or "consistent with a focal epilepsy"?
2. A 7-year-old with daily staring spells has a normal awake record. Hyperventilation is listed as "attempted." What must the report say?
3. Give two reasons to state medications and sedation in the report.

*Answers.* (1) The first is description; the second is clinical correlation. (2) Whether hyperventilation was adequate: poor effort weakens the negative finding for absence epilepsy, so the report should suggest repeating it. (3) Sedatives add beta activity and can alter sleep; antiseizure drugs can suppress discharges and slow the background.

**Sign-off:** the resident writes five full reports that a faculty reader accepts with minor edits.

## Module 3. Artifacts

Children move, feed and cry through their recordings, and rhythmic artifact in the NICU and PICU can look like a seizure.

**Objectives**

- Recognize the artifacts experts rated most important for residents to know, led by ECG, eye blinks, electrode pop and muscle (Nascimento 2022).
- Recognize the rhythmic artifacts of infancy and critical care, and confirm them on video.
- Use field and physiology to decide whether a waveform is cerebral.

**Teaching notes**

| Artifact | What it looks like | How to confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Eye blink | Symmetric downward deflection at Fp1 and Fp2 in bipolar montages; the cornea is positive and rotates up | Frontal only, no posterior field; eyes on video |
| Lateral eye movement | Opposite polarity at F7 and F8; brief lateral rectus spikes at the start of each saccade | Mirror image across hemispheres |
| Electrode pop | Abrupt, very steep deflection confined to one electrode | No field in neighboring electrodes |
| Muscle | Very fast, spiky activity, often temporal, during chewing or crying | Disappears in sleep or with relaxation |
| ECG | Sharp transient time-locked to the QRS, often prominent in infants | Line up with the ECG channel |
| Pulse | Slow, rhythmic waves at one electrode, following each QRS with a short delay | Electrode over an artery; moving it removes the artifact |
| Sweat | Very slow, high-amplitude drift | Frontal or temporal, in a warm child |
| 60 Hz line noise | Thick monotonous band in one or more channels | Check impedance of those electrodes |
| Sucking, patting, rocking | Rhythmic activity at the rate of the movement | Video; stops when the movement stops |
| High-frequency oscillatory ventilation | Rhythmic activity at the ventilator rate across many channels | Does not evolve; matches the ventilator setting |
| Glossokinetic | Slow frontal and temporal waves with tongue movement | Occurs with talking, crying or feeding |

- A cerebral discharge has a plausible field that spreads to neighboring electrodes. An artifact usually does not (IFCN criterion 6; see Module 5).
- Rhythmic patterns from patting, sucking or ventilation can look like seizures. They do not evolve in frequency, morphology or location the way seizures do. Always check the video before calling a neonatal or ICU seizure.

**Read and watch**

- Learning EEG, [Artifacts](https://www.learningeeg.com/artifacts.html).
- eeg-training.com, [EEG Fundamentals](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg-fundamentals.html): the artifacts unit and the Artifact Hunter exercise.
- [EEGmaster](https://eegmaster.com/eegmaster/) atlas, Artifacts module (free account required).
- [eeg-training.com EEG Videos](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg_videos.html): the eye-movement and technical-artifact lessons.

**Practice**

1. A 3-week-old on high-frequency oscillatory ventilation shows 10 Hz rhythmic activity in every channel for 20 minutes, unchanged in frequency. Seizure or artifact?
2. A spike appears at T4 alone, with no deflection at F8, C4 or T6. What is it most likely to be?
3. A 4-month-old has rhythmic 2 Hz frontal waves during feeding. What do you check?

*Answers.* (1) Artifact: it is diffuse, matches a ventilator rate and does not evolve. Check the ventilator setting. (2) Electrode pop: no physiologic field. (3) The video for sucking, and whether the pattern stops when feeding stops.

**Sign-off:** the resident correctly labels ten artifacts on real records, including at least three from infants or the ICU.

## Module 4. Normal EEG from infancy to adolescence

A record is normal or abnormal only for age, so the resident must know when each normal feature appears and disappears.

**Objectives**

- Estimate the expected posterior dominant rhythm for any age from 3 months to adolescence.
- Recognize age-specific normal sleep and drowsy patterns, and know when each appears.
- Describe an age-appropriate response to hyperventilation and photic stimulation.

**Teaching notes: the maturation table**

| Feature | Normal age range or value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Posterior dominant rhythm, 3 to 4 months | Reactive occipital rhythm of 3.5 to 4.5 Hz in about 75% of infants | Current Practice chapter |
| Posterior dominant rhythm, 12 months | 5 to 6 Hz in about 70% (5 to 7 Hz in St. Louis) | Current Practice; St. Louis 2016 |
| Posterior dominant rhythm, 3 years | Mean 8 Hz in 82% (range 7.5 to 9.5 Hz) | Current Practice |
| Posterior dominant rhythm, 9 and 15 years | Mean 9 Hz by age 9 and 10 Hz by age 15 | Current Practice |
| Sleep spindles | Appear at about 2 to 3 months post-term; often asynchronous until about 18 to 24 months | Current Practice; St. Louis 2016 |
| Vertex waves | Often by 3 months, reliably by 6 months | Current Practice; St. Louis 2016 |
| Hypnagogic hypersynchrony | 3 to 5 Hz, 75 to 350 µV bursts in drowsiness; 3 months to 13 years, typically 4 to 9 years | Kane 2017 |
| Posterior slow waves of youth | Typically 4 to 25 years; about 25% of awake children aged 1 to 15 | Kane 2017; Current Practice |
| Photic driving | From about 6 months | St. Louis 2016 |
| Lambda waves | From about 3 to 5 years | St. Louis 2016 |
| Hyperventilation | Usable from about age 3; marked slowing during it is normal in children | St. Louis 2016 |

Sources give slightly different ages, because they studied different cohorts. Treat the table as a guide to what is typical, not as fixed cutoffs.

- **Drowsiness is the danger zone.** Hypnagogic hypersynchrony and sharp vertex waves in children can look epileptiform. Check the state first.
- **Posterior slow waves of youth** ride on the alpha rhythm and block with eye opening, like the rhythm they sit on.

**Read and watch**

- Learning EEG, [Normal Awake](https://www.learningeeg.com/normal-awake.html), [Normal Asleep](https://www.learningeeg.com/normal-asleep.html) and [Pediatric](https://www.learningeeg.com/pediatric.html) (the Infancy, Early Years and Adolescence sections), plus the pediatric pages of the [Atlas](https://www.learningeeg.com/atlas.html).
- eeg-training.com, [EEG Fundamentals](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg-fundamentals.html): the age-change board.
- [EEGmaster](https://eegmaster.com/eegmaster/) atlas, Pediatric Normal module.
- AES [EEG Essentials](https://aesnet.org/education/education/eeg-essentials): Pediatric EEG Basics (free with a myAES account).
- St. Louis and Frey, [AES introductory text and atlas](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK390356/), developmental chapter (free).

**Practice**

1. An 18-month-old has a reactive 4 Hz posterior rhythm. Normal for age?
2. A 10-year-old has 3 Hz posterior waves riding on a 9 Hz alpha rhythm, blocked by eye opening. What are they?
3. A drowsy 5-year-old has runs of diffuse, high-voltage 4 Hz rhythmic activity with no spikes. What is the likely explanation?

*Answers.* (1) Slow: 5 to 6 Hz is expected by 12 months. Look for a cause, and check that the child was fully awake. (2) Posterior slow waves of youth, a normal finding. (3) Hypnagogic hypersynchrony.

**Sign-off:** the resident reads ten normal records spanning infancy to adolescence and states the expected posterior rhythm for each age before looking.

## Module 5. Normal variants and how not to overcall

Overreading sharply contoured normal activity as epileptiform is a documented route to misdiagnosing epilepsy, so this module teaches a rule for calling a discharge, not just a list of variants.

**Objectives**

- Recognize the benign variants, and know which ages they belong to.
- Apply the IFCN criteria before calling any transient epileptiform.
- Explain why a single discharge in a child without seizures does not establish epilepsy.

**Teaching notes**

| Variant | Typical age | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Hypnagogic hypersynchrony | 3 months to 13 years | Diffuse rhythmic 3 to 5 Hz bursts in drowsiness |
| Posterior slow waves of youth | 4 to 25 years | Slow waves riding on alpha, blocked by eye opening |
| 14 and 6 Hz positive bursts | Peak in adolescence | 13 to 17 Hz and/or 5 to 7 Hz positive spikes, posterior temporal, drowsiness and light sleep |
| 6 Hz spike-and-wave (phantom) | Adolescents and adults | Brief 4 to 7 Hz, low-amplitude spike-wave |
| Small sharp spikes (BETS) | Adults | Under 50 ms and 50 µV, temporal, drowsiness and light sleep |
| Wicket waves | Older adults | Arciform temporal waves, no after-going slow wave |

Ages from Kane 2017 and St. Louis 2016. Wickets and small sharp spikes are rare in young children, but residents meet them in adolescents and parents.

**The IFCN criteria.** A transient is epileptiform if it meets at least 4 of these 6 (Kane 2017):

1. Di- or triphasic, with a sharp or spiky morphology.
2. Different duration from the ongoing background, shorter or longer.
3. Asymmetric waveform: a sharply rising phase and a more slowly decaying one, or the reverse.
4. Followed by an after-going slow wave.
5. Disrupts the surrounding background.
6. A scalp distribution of negative and positive potentials that implies a source in the brain.

In a validation study of 100 patients, requiring at least 5 of the 6 gave 95.7% specificity and 81.5% sensitivity. The authors judged 5 the best threshold for clinical use (Kural 2020).

- **Discharges occur in healthy children.** In a prospective study of healthy children, 6.5% had epileptiform discharges and 4.2% had rolandic discharges; earlier studies reported 3.5% to 5% (Borusiak 2010).
- **What gets overread.** In patients misdiagnosed with epilepsy, the overread findings were mostly nonspecific fluctuations or sharply contoured background, often temporal. Wickets, hypnagogic hypersynchrony and hyperventilation slowing were also overread (Benbadis 2003; Benbadis 2008).

**Read and watch**

- Learning EEG, [Normal Variants](https://www.learningeeg.com/normal-variants.html).
- eeg-training.com, [EEG Fundamentals](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg-fundamentals.html): the normal-variants unit.
- [EEGmaster](https://eegmaster.com/eegmaster/) atlas, Benign EEG Variants module.
- Kural 2020 in [Neurology](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000009439).

**Practice**

1. A 15-year-old has brief, positive, 14 Hz posterior temporal bursts in light sleep. What is it, and what goes in the report?
2. A sharp transient is spiky and biphasic, and briefer than the background, but has no after-going slow wave, does not disrupt the background and has no field. How many IFCN criteria does it meet?
3. A 6-year-old referred for headaches has occasional right centrotemporal spikes and has never had a seizure. Does she have epilepsy?

*Answers.* (1) 14 and 6 Hz positive bursts, a normal variant. Describe them as a variant, or leave them out of the impression, rather than calling them abnormal. (2) Three at most (criteria 1, 2 and possibly 3): do not call it epileptiform. (3) No. Centrotemporal discharges occur in about 4% of healthy children, and epilepsy is a clinical diagnosis.

**Sign-off:** on ten sharp transients chosen by faculty, the resident scores each against the six criteria and agrees with the faculty call on at least eight.

## Module 6. Neonatal EEG I: maturation by postmenstrual age

A neonatal record is read against postmenstrual age, not days of life, because a pattern normal at 30 weeks is abnormal at term. None of the three core websites covers this in depth, so this module leans on dedicated neonatal material.

**Objectives**

- Calculate postmenstrual age and state what continuity, synchrony and graphoelements to expect at it.
- Identify wake, active sleep and quiet sleep in a term neonate.
- Recognize dysmature features: a record that looks younger than its postmenstrual age.

**Teaching notes**

Postmenstrual age = gestational age at birth + chronological age. A baby born at 28 weeks and now 6 weeks old is read as 34 weeks.

| Feature | When it is normal | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Discontinuity (tracé discontinu) | Extreme prematurity; persists in quiet sleep to about 34 weeks | Wake and active sleep become continuous from about 30 to 34 weeks |
| Hypersynchrony | Before about 27 to 29 weeks | Bursts are synchronous across hemispheres |
| Interhemispheric synchrony | Only about 70% at 29 to 30 weeks, nearly 100% again by term | Synchronous = bursts within 1.5 s of each other (Tsuchida 2013) |
| Temporal theta (sawtooth) | From about 24 weeks; gone by about 32 to 34 weeks | Rhythmic temporal theta |
| Delta brushes | Appear at about 24 to 28 weeks, peak at about 31 to 34 weeks, gone by 40 to 44 weeks | Fast activity riding on delta waves |
| Frontal sharp transients (encoches frontales) | From about 34 to 35 weeks; normal to about 44 to 46 weeks | Blunt, often bilateral frontal sharp waves |
| Tracé alternant (quiet sleep) | Appears at about 34 to 37 weeks; gone by about 44 to 46 weeks | Bursts alternating with lower-voltage interburst periods |
| Reactivity | From about 30 to 32 weeks; reliable by 35 to 36 weeks | Change in the background with stimulation |
| Sleep cycle | 30 to 50 minutes below 35 weeks; 50 to 65 minutes above 35 weeks | Tsuchida 2013 |

**Longest normal interburst interval.** It shortens with age: about 60 s at 24 weeks, 40 s at 26 weeks, 20 s at 28 weeks, 10 to 20 s at 30 to 34 weeks, about 10 s at 34 to 36 weeks, and about 6 s at term (Learning EEG; consistent with Medscape). The French consensus reports wider maxima, for example 12 to 76 s at 24 to 26 weeks (André 2010). Some texts give median rather than maximum intervals, which explains much of the disagreement.

Ages in the table are consensus ranges; sources differ by 1 to 4 weeks for most features (André 2010; St. Louis 2016; Current Practice chapter). Anterior slow dysrhythmia appears near term, mostly in the shift from active to quiet sleep; no source gave firm age limits.

**Read and watch**

- Learning EEG, [Neonatal](https://www.learningeeg.com/neonatal.html) chapter and the neonatal pages of the [Atlas](https://www.learningeeg.com/atlas.html).
- AES [EEG Essentials](https://aesnet.org/education/education/eeg-essentials): Neonatal EEG Basics (free with a myAES account).
- [Neonatal Brain Monitoring course](https://nncceducation.thinkific.com/courses/Neonatal-Brain-monitoring), Calgary Newborn Neuro-Intensive Care Program: 13 lessons, about 7.5 hours, free.
- StatPearls, [EEG Neonatal Visual Analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK563258/) (free).
- For reference: André 2010 glossary; Mizrahi and Hrachovy, *Atlas of Neonatal Electroencephalography*.

**Practice**

1. A baby born at 30 weeks is now 8 weeks old. What postmenstrual age do you read the record against?
2. A term newborn's quiet sleep shows bursts alternating with lower-voltage periods of 3 to 5 s. Normal?
3. A baby at 42 weeks postmenstrual age has abundant delta brushes in every state. What does that suggest?

*Answers.* (1) 38 weeks. (2) Yes: tracé alternant, the normal quiet-sleep pattern at term. (3) A dysmature record: delta brushes should have largely gone by 40 to 44 weeks. Report it as immature for postmenstrual age.

**Sign-off:** the resident reads five neonatal records of different postmenstrual ages and estimates the age from the tracing to within 2 weeks.

## Module 7. Neonatal EEG II: abnormal background, seizures and NICU monitoring

Most neonatal seizures cannot be diagnosed at the bedside, so the resident must be able to grade a background and call a seizure on EEG in ACNS terms.

**Objectives**

- Grade a neonatal background using the ACNS neonatal categories.
- Define a neonatal electrographic seizure and neonatal status epilepticus, and how they differ from the pediatric and adult definitions.
- Explain why clinical observation alone misses and overcalls neonatal seizures, and when continuous monitoring is indicated.

**Teaching notes: ACNS neonatal terminology (Tsuchida 2013)**

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Continuous | Less than 2 s of attenuation below 25 µV |
| Discontinuous | Interburst intervals of at least 2 s, attenuated below 25 to 50 µV |
| Excessively discontinuous | Interburst intervals too long or too low for postmenstrual age; bursts still contain some normal features. No numeric cutoff |
| Burst suppression | Invariant, abnormally composed bursts; interburst voltage below 5 µV; no variability or reactivity |
| Borderline low voltage | Continuous, with some normal features, but persistently 10 to under 25 µV |
| Low voltage suppressed | No normal features; baseline below 10 µV |
| Seizure | A sudden, repetitive, evolving pattern of at least 2 µV lasting at least 10 s |
| Brief rhythmic discharges (BRDs) | Evolving rhythmic patterns lasting under 10 s |
| Neonatal status epilepticus | Seizures summing to at least 50% of any 1-hour epoch |

**ILAE 2021 neonatal classification (Pressler 2021).**

- Video-EEG is the gold standard for diagnosis.
- Neonatal seizures are treated as focal in onset, so there is no focal versus generalized split.
- Seizures are typed by their predominant feature: motor (automatisms, clonic, epileptic spasms, myoclonic, tonic), non-motor (autonomic, behavior arrest) or sequential.
- Electrographic-only seizures are a category of their own. The paper cites 50% to 80% of seizures being electrographic-only in selected populations with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy.

**Why bedside observation fails.** In a study of 51 monitored neonates, 526 electrographic seizures occurred in 9 babies. Only 34% had any clinical sign on video, and staff recognized 9%. Of 177 events staff suspected were seizures, only 27% had an EEG correlate (Murray 2008).

**When to monitor.** For high-risk neonates, ACNS recommends 24 hours of conventional EEG to screen for seizures, at least 1 hour to assess the background, and monitoring until 24 hours seizure-free after a seizure (Shellhaas 2011). A 2025 ACNS guideline updates the indications, with conditional recommendations because the evidence is of very low certainty (Wusthoff 2025).

**Read and watch**

- ACNS neonatal terminology, [free PDF](https://www.acns.org/pdf/guidelines/Guideline-16.pdf); ACNS neonatal monitoring guideline, [free PDF](https://www.acns.org/pdf/guidelines/Guideline-13.pdf).
- Learning EEG, [Neonatal](https://www.learningeeg.com/neonatal.html): the epileptiform abnormalities section and its "find the sharp transient" exercise.
- [Neonatal Brain Monitoring course](https://nncceducation.thinkific.com/courses/Neonatal-Brain-monitoring) (free): the seizure and amplitude-integrated EEG lessons.
- For reference: Sansevere and Harrar, *Atlas of Pediatric and Neonatal ICU EEG*.

**Practice**

1. A cooled term neonate has rhythmic 1 Hz sharp waves at C3 that speed to 2 Hz and spread to T3 over 40 s. No clinical change is seen. Is it a seizure?
2. Over one hour, seizures total 34 minutes. Is this neonatal status epilepticus?
3. A term baby's background is invariant, with bursts separated by periods under 5 µV, and it does not change with stimulation. Which ACNS category?

*Answers.* (1) Yes. It is repetitive, evolves in frequency and location, and lasts at least 10 s: an electrographic-only seizure. (2) Yes: 34 minutes is at least 50% of the hour. (3) Burst suppression.

**Sign-off:** the resident grades five neonatal backgrounds and identifies seizures on two cEEG studies, agreeing with the faculty read.

## Module 8. Interictal epileptiform and non-epileptiform abnormalities

Once a transient passes the Module 5 test, the resident must describe it precisely and separate epileptiform findings from slowing, which points to a different set of causes.

**Objectives**

- Describe an epileptiform discharge by type, location, field, prevalence and state.
- Distinguish focal from generalized discharges, and recognize secondary bilateral synchrony as a possibility.
- Recognize focal and diffuse slowing, intermittent rhythmic delta and asymmetries, and state what each suggests.

**Teaching notes**

- **Spike or sharp wave.** By the IFCN glossary, a spike lasts 20 to under 70 ms and a sharp wave 70 to 200 ms (Kane 2017). The distinction has little clinical weight; location and field matter more.
- **Generalized discharges.** Spike-wave, polyspike-wave and slow spike-wave are described by frequency and by whether they are activated by sleep, hyperventilation or photic stimulation (Module 9).
- **Sleep activates.** Many focal and generalized discharges appear only in drowsiness and sleep. A record without sleep is a weaker negative.
- **Focal polymorphic delta** that is continuous and does not react suggests a structural lesion on that side.
- **Diffuse slowing** of the background for age suggests encephalopathy, from metabolic, toxic, post-ictal or diffuse structural causes.
- **Intermittent rhythmic delta.** Occipital intermittent rhythmic delta (OIRDA) is a childhood pattern, often seen with absence epilepsy. Frontal intermittent rhythmic delta (FIRDA) is usually a nonspecific sign of encephalopathy.
- **Asymmetry.** A lower-voltage side can mean cortical dysfunction or something between the cortex and the electrodes, such as a subdural collection or scalp swelling. A breach rhythm over a skull defect is higher in voltage and sharper, and is not epileptiform.

**Read and watch**

- Learning EEG, [Non-Epileptiform](https://www.learningeeg.com/nonepileptiform.html) and [Epileptiform Discharges](https://www.learningeeg.com/epileptiform.html).
- eeg-training.com, [EEG Fundamentals](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg-fundamentals.html): the interictal patterns unit.
- [eeg-training.com EEG Videos](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg_videos.html): the interictal abnormalities, focal slowing and diffuse slowing lessons.
- [EEGmaster](https://eegmaster.com/eegmaster/) atlas, Nonepileptiform and Interictal Epileptiform modules.

**Practice**

1. A 9-year-old has continuous, irregular 1 to 2 Hz delta over the left temporal region that does not change with state. What does it suggest, and what do you recommend?
2. A 6-year-old has runs of rhythmic 3 Hz delta over both occipital regions, which block with eye opening. What pattern, and what should you look for in the rest of the record?
3. A child has lower voltage over the right hemisphere after a head injury. Name two explanations.

*Answers.* (1) A structural lesion; recommend neuroimaging if it has not been done. (2) OIRDA; look for generalized 3 Hz spike-wave, especially during hyperventilation. (3) Cortical dysfunction on the right, or fluid or swelling between cortex and electrodes, such as a subdural hematoma.

**Sign-off:** the resident describes ten abnormal records in full, with each finding placed correctly as epileptiform or non-epileptiform.

## Module 9. EEG of the pediatric epilepsy syndromes

The ILAE 2022 syndrome definitions each list mandatory EEG features, alerts and exclusions, so the EEG often confirms or rules out a syndrome outright.

**Objectives**

- State the mandatory EEG features of the common infant and childhood syndromes.
- Know which findings argue against a syndrome, for example no spike-wave in an untreated child after adequate hyperventilation.
- Recommend the right study for the question: sleep for SeLECTS and spike-wave activation in sleep, hyperventilation for absence, and a recorded event for spasms.

**Teaching notes (ILAE 2022)**

| Syndrome | Key EEG features | Pitfalls |
|---|---|---|
| Early infantile DEE (EIDEE) | Burst suppression or multifocal discharges, with diffuse slowing | — |
| Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS) | Interictal: hypsarrhythmia, or multifocal or focal discharges. Ictal: a high-amplitude generalized wave, then low-amplitude fast activity (electrodecrement) | Hypsarrhythmia is not required and may be absent early; do not delay treatment for it. A normal EEG during recorded spasms argues against IESS and should prompt you to reconsider the diagnosis |
| Childhood absence epilepsy | Generalized 3 Hz (range 2.5 to 4 Hz) spike-wave, provoked by hyperventilation in most untreated patients; photic stimulation triggers it in 21% | No spike-wave after 3 minutes of well-performed hyperventilation in an untreated child makes it very unlikely (an alert in ILAE 2022; exclusionary on epilepsydiagnosis.org) |
| Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy | Generalized 3 to 5.5 Hz polyspike-wave and spike-wave; photoparoxysmal response in more than a third | Discharges can look focal or asymmetric |
| SeLECTS (self-limited epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes) | High-amplitude centrotemporal sharp-and-slow waves, activated in drowsiness and sleep, with a horizontal dipole (frontal positive, temporo-parietal negative) | Also seen in healthy children (Module 5) |
| SeLEAS (Panayiotopoulos) | Multifocal, high-voltage spike or sharp-and-slow waves, often posterior, shifting between recordings | Do not read the shifting foci as a structural lesion |
| Lennox-Gastaut syndrome | Generalized slow spike-wave below 2.5 Hz, and generalized paroxysmal fast activity (10 Hz or more), often in sleep | Both are required; one alone is not enough |
| DEE-SWAS and EE-SWAS (formerly ESES/CSWS) | Slow (about 1.5 to 2 Hz) spike-wave markedly activated in NREM sleep | ILAE sets no spike-wave index threshold; the old 85% cutoff is not required |

Sources: Zuberi 2022 (neonatal and infantile onset), Specchio 2022 (childhood onset), Hirsch E 2022 (idiopathic generalized) and the ILAE site [epilepsydiagnosis.org](https://www.epilepsydiagnosis.org/).

**Read and watch**

- Learning EEG, [Pediatric](https://www.learningeeg.com/pediatric.html): the Ohtahara (EIDEE), hypsarrhythmia and infantile spasms, LGS, SeLECTS, absence, ESES and JME sections.
- [EEGmaster](https://eegmaster.com/eegmaster/) atlas, Pediatric Epilepsy module.
- eeg-training.com, [ILAE Classification](https://www.eeg-training.com/ilae_classification.html): the 2022 syndrome lookup.
- AES [EEG Essentials](https://aesnet.org/education/education/eeg-essentials): Pediatric Epilepsy Spectrums.
- [epilepsydiagnosis.org](https://www.epilepsydiagnosis.org/): the EEG tab of each syndrome page.

**Practice**

1. A 7-month-old with clusters of flexor spasms has a chaotic, high-voltage record with multifocal spikes. A spasm shows a generalized slow wave followed by low-voltage fast activity. What is the diagnosis?
2. An untreated 6-year-old with frequent staring does 3 minutes of vigorous hyperventilation with no spike-wave. What does this mean for childhood absence epilepsy?
3. A 5-year-old with regression has spike-wave that is sparse awake but nearly continuous in NREM sleep. Which syndrome family should you consider, and does it need an 85% spike-wave index?

*Answers.* (1) Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, with hypsarrhythmia and an ictal electrodecremental response. (2) It makes childhood absence epilepsy very unlikely if the hyperventilation was well performed; ILAE 2022 treats it as an alert to reconsider the diagnosis. (3) DEE-SWAS or EE-SWAS. No: ILAE 2022 requires marked activation in sleep, not a specific index.

**Sign-off:** the resident identifies the syndrome-defining features in at least one record each of IESS, an absence epilepsy and SeLECTS.

## Module 10. Seizures and status epilepticus on EEG

A seizure on EEG is recognized by evolution, not by any single waveform, and the ACNS thresholds turn that into a definition residents can apply.

**Objectives**

- Recognize evolution in frequency, morphology and location, and the common scalp ictal patterns in children.
- Apply the ACNS 2021 definitions of electrographic and electroclinical seizures and status epilepticus.
- Know which seizures a scalp EEG can miss.

**Teaching notes: ACNS 2021 definitions (Hirsch 2021)**

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Electrographic seizure | Epileptiform discharges averaging more than 2.5 Hz for at least 10 s, or any pattern with definite evolution lasting at least 10 s |
| Electrographic status epilepticus | Electrographic seizure for at least 10 continuous minutes, or for at least 20% of any 60-minute period |
| Electroclinical seizure | Any pattern with a definite clinical correlate time-locked to it, or with EEG and clinical improvement after a parenteral antiseizure medication |
| Electroclinical status epilepticus | At least 10 continuous minutes or 20% of any hour; only 5 continuous minutes if there is bilateral tonic-clonic activity |
| Evolution | At least 2 unequivocal, sequential changes in frequency (by at least 0.5 Hz), morphology or location, each lasting at least 3 cycles |

- **Neonates are different.** A neonatal seizure needs only evolution and 10 s. Neonatal status is 50% of an hour, not 20% (Module 7).
- **Common patterns.** Focal seizures often start as rhythmic theta or alpha that slows and spreads, or as focal attenuation. Absence seizures start and stop abruptly with 3 Hz spike-wave. Tonic seizures and spasms show generalized fast activity or electrodecrement.
- **Scalp EEG can miss seizures.** Focal aware seizures, and some deep or frontal seizures, may have no clear scalp correlate. A normal EEG during a stereotyped event does not always exclude a seizure, so correlate with the semiology.
- **Muscle can hide the onset.** Look for the first change before the muscle artifact starts, and at the post-ictal slowing afterward.

**Read and watch**

- Learning EEG, [Seizures](https://www.learningeeg.com/seizures.html).
- eeg-training.com, [EEG Fundamentals](https://www.eeg-training.com/eeg-fundamentals.html): the seizure-recognition unit and seizure-evolution playback.
- [EEGmaster](https://eegmaster.com/eegmaster/) atlas, Ictal EEG module, and its [seizure atlas](https://eegmaster.com/seizure-atlas).
- ACNS 2021 terminology, [free PDF](https://www.acns.org/UserFiles/file/ACNSStandardizedCriticalCareEEGTerminology_rev2021.pdf).

**Practice**

1. A 9-year-old shows 2 Hz generalized spike-wave for 12 s with no clinical change. Is this an electrographic seizure?
2. A PICU patient has 3-minute electrographic seizures, 5 per hour. Does this meet electrographic status epilepticus?
3. A 12-year-old reports a rising epigastric sensation during the recording, with no EEG change. Does this exclude a focal seizure?

*Answers.* (1) Not by frequency, since 2 Hz is not above 2.5 Hz. It qualifies only if it shows definite evolution; otherwise it falls in the ictal-interictal continuum (Module 11). (2) Yes: 15 minutes is 25% of the hour, more than 20%. (3) No. Focal aware seizures often have no scalp correlate.

**Sign-off:** the resident identifies onset, evolution and offset in five recorded seizures, including at least one generalized and one focal.

## Module 11. Critical care EEG in children

About a third of critically ill children on continuous EEG have electrographic seizures, and many have no clinical sign, so the resident on call must be able to name ICU patterns in ACNS terms and know when they matter.

**Objectives**

- Name any rhythmic or periodic pattern with the ACNS 2021 main terms, modifiers and frequency qualifiers.
- Apply the ictal-interictal continuum criteria, and know what to do with a pattern that falls on it.
- Summarize the pediatric evidence linking seizure burden to outcome.
- Use quantitative trends to find events, then confirm them on the raw EEG.

**Teaching notes: ACNS 2021 terminology (Hirsch 2021)**

- **Main term 1** gives the location: generalized (G), lateralized (L), bilateral independent (BI), unilateral independent (UI) or multifocal (Mf).
- **Main term 2** gives the pattern: periodic discharges (PDs), rhythmic delta activity (RDA) or spike-and-wave (SW). Combined, they give GPDs, LPDs, BIPDs, GRDA, LRDA and so on.
- **Plus modifiers:** +F superimposed fast activity; +R superimposed rhythmic delta (PDs only); +S superimposed sharp waves or spikes (RDA only).
- **Prevalence:** continuous at least 90% of the record; abundant 50% to 89%; frequent 10% to 49%; occasional 1% to 9%; rare under 1%.
- **BIRDs** (brief potentially ictal rhythmic discharges): rhythmic activity above 4 Hz, lasting 0.5 to under 10 s, with no clinical correlate.
- **Ictal-interictal continuum.** A pattern that is not a seizure but is one of the following: PDs or SW at more than 1 and up to 2.5 Hz; PDs or SW at 0.5 to 1 Hz with a plus modifier or fluctuation; or lateralized RDA above 1 Hz with a plus modifier or fluctuation.
- **Fluctuation versus evolution.** Fluctuation is 3 or more changes, no more than 1 minute apart, that do not meet the evolution criteria in Module 10.

**The pediatric evidence**

| Study | Children | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Abend 2013 | 550 at 11 centers | Electrographic seizures in 30%; exclusively electrographic in 36% of those; electrographic status in 38% of those with seizures, and associated with in-hospital death |
| Topjian 2013 | 200 | Electrographic status in 21.5%; associated with death (odds ratio 5.1) and neurologic decline (odds ratio 17.3). Seizures without status did not independently predict outcome |
| Payne 2014 | 259 | Seizures in 36%. Neurologic decline rose sharply above a maximum hourly seizure burden of 20% (12 minutes per hour); odds ratio 1.13 for each 1% rise |

Indications for continuous EEG in critically ill children follow the ACNS consensus statement (Herman 2015).

**Quantitative EEG.** Spectrograms, amplitude-integrated EEG and asymmetry trends help find seizures in long records. Always confirm a trend change on the raw EEG before calling it.

**Read and watch**

- ACNS 2021 terminology, [free PDF](https://www.acns.org/UserFiles/file/ACNSStandardizedCriticalCareEEGTerminology_rev2021.pdf).
- ACNS [CCEMRC training and certification](https://www.acns.org/research/critical-care-eeg-monitoring-research-consortium-ccemrc/education): free slides, then a self-assessment of 50 questions and 25 EEG examples, with a certificate at 80% or above.
- eeg-training.com, [ACNS Terminology](https://www.eeg-training.com/acns_criteria.html): the 2012-to-2021 mapping, pattern-name builder and strip-reading drills.
- Learning EEG, [Rhythmicity & Periodicity](https://www.learningeeg.com/rhythmicity-periodicity.html), with its interactive ictal-interictal continuum diagram.
- [EEGmaster](https://eegmaster.com/eegmaster/) atlas, ICU Background, ICU Patterns, ICU Ictal and Quantitative EEG modules.
- For reference: Sansevere and Harrar, *Atlas of Pediatric and Neonatal ICU EEG*.

**Practice**

1. A child after cardiac arrest has generalized periodic discharges at 1.5 Hz, abundant, with no evolution. What is the ACNS name, and where does it sit?
2. Left-sided rhythmic delta at 1.5 Hz with superimposed sharp waves, lasting minutes. Name it, and say whether it meets the ictal-interictal continuum.
3. A 6-second run of 6 Hz rhythmic activity at T4, with the same location as the patient's spikes. What is it?

*Answers.* (1) Abundant GPDs at 1.5 Hz: on the ictal-interictal continuum, because they are above 1 and no more than 2.5 Hz. (2) LRDA+S at 1.5 Hz: yes, lateralized RDA above 1 Hz with a plus modifier. (3) A definite BIRD: rhythmic, above 4 Hz, under 10 s, and in the location of the patient's discharges.

**Sign-off:** the resident passes the ACNS CCEMRC certification test and reviews two pediatric cEEG days with faculty, naming every pattern.

## Module 12. Supervised reading and final sign-off

Self-study builds vocabulary, but reading skill comes from pre-reading real studies and comparing each read with an attending's. So this module runs alongside the others from the first week.

**How it works**

- The resident pre-reads a study and writes a full draft report before seeing the official one.
- The attending reviews the draft, and the resident logs the study, the draft impression and any disagreement.
- Each month, the resident and a faculty reader review the log for recurring errors, such as overcalling (Module 5) or missed artifact (Module 3).

**How many studies?** There is no validated minimum. In the 2024 survey, 93% of child neurology programs set none, and programs required a mean of 7.3 weeks of EEG training (Katyal 2024). Nascimento 2023 concluded that more than 8 weeks, ideally more than 12, is needed for minimal routine-EEG competency (as summarized in Katyal 2024). Set a local number and track it. A reasonable starting mix, offered as a suggestion rather than a standard:

- 60 routine studies, at least 20 of them in children under 2.
- 10 neonatal studies across a range of postmenstrual ages.
- 10 days of pediatric or neonatal continuous EEG.
- At least one recorded example each of IESS, an absence epilepsy and SeLECTS.

**Objective testing**

- [EEGmaster](https://eegmaster.com/curriculum_intro) has six milestone-aligned competency tests of 25 questions each. A trainee can name the program director as "Program Officer," who can then track results.
- The ACNS CCEMRC certification covers the ICU (Module 11).

**Final sign-off checklist**

- [ ] Module 1: localized five discharges in bipolar and referential montages
- [ ] Module 2: five full reports accepted with minor edits
- [ ] Module 3: labeled ten artifacts, at least three from infants or the ICU
- [ ] Module 4: read ten normal records from infancy to adolescence
- [ ] Module 5: scored ten sharp transients against the IFCN criteria, agreeing on at least eight
- [ ] Module 6: estimated postmenstrual age within 2 weeks on five neonatal records
- [ ] Module 7: graded five neonatal backgrounds and identified seizures on two cEEG studies
- [ ] Module 8: described ten abnormal records in full
- [ ] Module 9: identified syndrome-defining features of IESS, an absence epilepsy and SeLECTS
- [ ] Module 10: marked onset, evolution and offset in five recorded seizures
- [ ] Module 11: passed the ACNS CCEMRC certification and reviewed two pediatric cEEG days
- [ ] Module 12: completed the local reading target and all six EEGmaster competency tests
